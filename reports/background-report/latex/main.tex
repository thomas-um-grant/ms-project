\chapter{Introduction}
\section{LLMs and VLMs revolution}
%\textit{Rapid progress in LLMs (GPT, Llama, Mistral, etc.) and VLMs (CLIP, BLIP2, PaliGemma)} \\
In 2017, the introduction of the transformer architecture\cite{Transformer} at the NeurIPS conference sparked widespread academic and industry interest in large language models (LLMs). The transformer mechanism fundamentally reshaped sequence modeling. This breakthrough drove a series of state-of-the-art models the following year. Among them were BERT\cite{BERT} presented by Google, which excelled at language understanding for classification tasks, and GPT\cite{GPT} proposed by OpenAI which showcased fluent language generation.

Intensified research effort lead to a computer vision breakthrough as well a couple years later. OpenAI introduced a novel approach to learning visual representation that scaled effectively accross tasks named CLIP\cite{CLIP}, which popularized vision language models (VLMs). Unlike earlier pioneer research DeViSE\cite{DeViSE} which lacked contextual language modeling, or VilBERT\cite{ViLBERT} which required task-specific fine-tuning preventing it to generalize well, training on large-scale image-text pairs using a contrastive objective enabled CLIP to remain robust and performant across a wide range of visual tasks.

%\textit{Empowerment: LLMs excel at text generation, reasoning, and knowledge tasks} \\
%\textit{VLMs enable understanding and generation across text and images} \\
The surge of foundational models\cite{FoundationModels} gained further public attention in 2022 with the release of ChatGPT\cite{IntroducingChatGPT}, Stable Diffusion\cite{StableDiffusion} and DALL-E\cite{DALL.E-2}. These generative models and their optimized variants are now widely deployed across industry. Their remarkable capacity to generate coherent text and high-fidelity images, as well as perform reasoning and handle knowledge-intensive tasks transformed professional workflows\cite{FoundationModelsInBigBusiness}.

%\textit{Challenges:} \\
%\textit{Limited context window and memory} \\
%\textit{Hallucination and lack of grounding in external facts} \\
%\textit{Inability to access up-to-date or external knowledge} \\
%\textit{Poor performance on domain-specific or multimodal tasks} \\
%\textit{Difficulty with reasoning over complex, structured, or multi-hop information} \\
Despite their impressive performances, foundational models face critical limitations in domains where factually grounded, up-to-date information generation is essential. A phenomenon known as "hallucination"\cite{Hallucinations} in which models generate plausible but incorrect or fabricated outputs raises concerns about their reliability. These models are trained on static datasets bounded in time, and infer results with a limited context window. As a result, they often underperform on domain-specific tasks, especially when reasoning over complex, structured, or multi-hop information\cite{ReasoningOrReciting}. These limitations have motivated research into methods which integrate retrieval mechanisms to augment factuality and reasoning capabalities with external knowledge sources, commonly referred to as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) frameworks.

\section{Rise of RAG}
%\textit{General introduction:} \\
%\textit{Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG): combines LLMs with external retrieval to ground responses in relevant documents} \\
The original Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system was introduced by the Facebook AI Research (FAIR) team in 2020\cite{FAIRRAG}. They proposed a framework which integrates external knowledge to enhance the output quality. Additionally, it enables traceability, identifying the provenance of the knowledge behind generated content. The RAG framework demonstrated this approach produces more factual and specific responses compared to standalone LLMs. Since then, a growing ecosystem of RAGs has emerged\cite{RAGSurvey}, exploring trade-offs tailored for specific use cases.

%\textit{Pipeline: retrieve relevant chunks, then feed to LLM for answer generation} \\
Despite the variability accross RAG implementations, the core methodology remains consistent. A standard pipeline consists of three sequential stages: retrieve, augment, and generate. First, the retrieval process involves fetching pertinent information from an external knowledge base named "corpus". To do so, it encodes the user query to obtain its vector representation, which is then used to search for similar vectors among pre-indexed corpora. The top-N most similar matches are then retrieved from the corpus. The retrieved information is then provided alongside the original query to the foundational model. In this pioneering research, the FAIR team demonstrate that augmenting the original query with retrieved relevant context improves the answer quality in terms of factuality and specificity. In the final step, the foundational model generates a response conditioned on the augmented query provided.

%\textit{Overview of its potential:} \\
%\textit{Improves factuality, reduces hallucination} \\
%\textit{Enables up-to-date and domain-specific knowledge access} \\
%\textit{Supports multi-hop, complex, and multimodal queries} \\
%\textit{Can use text, images, graphs, or hybrid retrieval} \\
The utility of RAG frameworks extends beyond reducing hallucinations and addressing limitations in generalization. It also enables a foundational model to stay relevant over-time without additional costly training\cite{TrainingFrontierAICost}, as up-to-date or new domain-specific information can be continuously integrated into the knowledge base. Recent developments in domain-specific RAG pipelines have sparked interests in the health care industry to assist medical professionals, although serious limitations are highlighted\cite{RAGinHealthCare}. SentimentCareBot\cite{SentimentCareBot}, a RAG Chatbot for mental health support may help address the growing demand for accessible specialist support, such as psychologists and counselors.
RAG systems are also recognized for their ability to perform retrieval across mutliple modalities\cite{AskInAnyModality} such as texts and images, leveraging various data structures including vectors and graphs databases to organize the corpus. These techniques create new opportunities for practical applications, decoupling the utility of foundational models from their training data.

\section{Attention with Transformers}
%\textit{What are transformers and how they are used for query answering.} \\

% The transformer architecture, introduced in 2017\cite{AttentionIsAllYouNeed}, revolutionized sequence modeling by replacing recurrence with a self-attention mechanism that enables each input token to attend to all others in the sequence. It means the transformer encoder learns contextual representations through a multi-head attention layer, where queries (Q), keys (K), and values (V) are projected from the same input embedding. The scaled dot-product attention function $Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}})V$ allows each token to weigh information from every other token, which effectively models long-range dependencies without the sequential bottleneck of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), its predecessor.
% Transformer variants are specialized for different tasks: encoder-only models (e.g., BERT \cite{BERT}) excel at classification and masked language modeling; encoder-decoder models (e.g., T5 \cite{T5}) handle sequence-to-sequence tasks like translation and summarization; decoder-only models (e.g., GPT \cite{GPT}) focus on causal language modeling and open-ended generation. Vision transformers (e.g., ViT \cite{ViT}) apply self-attention to image patches for tasks like classification and detection. Multimodal models (e.g., ViLBERT \cite{ViLBERT}, CLIP \cite{CLIP}, DALL·E \cite{DALLE}) extend attention across text and images for captioning, retrieval, and generation.
% In query-answering systems, especially in modern RAG pipeline, the transformer's ability to capture complex token interactions underpins both retrieval and generation stages. Dense retrievers often use transformer encoders to map queries and documents into a shared embedding space, where nearest-neighbor search retrieves the most relevant passages. These passages, concatenated with the original query, are then passed to a decoder (or encoder-decoder) to generate grounded answers, benefiting from the transformer's parallel attention over both query and retrieved context.

%\textit{The limitations of LLMS to answer queries on domain specific and contextual data.} \\

% Despite their versatility, foundational models (e.g. LLMs) face several limitations when used for domain-specific or context-dependent query answering:
% Static knowledge: Trained on fixed data snapshots, LLMs lack awareness of events post-training, making them unable to deliver up-to-date information.
% Shallow domain expertise: While they capture broad factual patterns, LLMs often struggle with deep understanding in technical or niche domains, leading to incomplete or generalized answers.
% Limited context window and memory: Fixed token limits restrict how much information can be processed at once, impairing performance on long conversations or multi-document tasks.
% Ambiguity and hallucinations: Vague prompts or gaps in input can lead LLMs to generate plausible but incorrect or fabricated responses, reducing reliability.
% No access to private/internal data: Out of the box, LLMs cannot retrieve information from proprietary databases or internal systems, limiting enterprise use without customization.
% Multi-step reasoning challenges: Tasks requiring sequential logic or multi-hop reasoning often exceed LLMs' capabilities, resulting in errors or inconsistencies.
% Compliance and accuracy risks: In regulated domains like healthcare or finance, uncontrolled outputs can pose legal or safety risks, necessitating external controls and validation.

The transformer architecture\ref{appendix:transformer}, introduced in 2017 \cite{AttentionIsAllYouNeed}, revolutionized sequence modeling by replacing recurrence with self-attention, allowing each token to contextualize itself with respect to all others in the sequence. This is achieved via multi-head attention using queries, keys, and values derived from the same input, enabling efficient modeling of long-range dependencies. Transformer variants serve distinct roles: encoder-only models (e.g., BERT \cite{BERT}) support classification and masked prediction; encoder-decoder models (e.g., T5 \cite{T5}) handle translation and summarization; and decoder-only models (e.g., GPT \cite{GPT}) are optimized for generative tasks. Extensions include vision transformers (e.g., ViT \cite{ViT}) and multimodal architectures (e.g., CLIP \cite{CLIP}, DALL·E \cite{DALL.E-2}).

Despite their versatility, foundational models still face challenges in domain-specific and context-dependent applications. These include static knowledge, limited depth in specialized domains, fixed context windows, susceptibility to hallucinations, inability to access private/internal data, difficulty with multi-step reasoning, and compliance risks in regulated environments. Such limitations have driven the adoption of RAG methods to ground outputs in external, up-to-date, and domain-relevant sources, enhancing accuracy and reducing hallucinations.

In RAG pipelines, transformers underpin both retrieval and generation: encoders map queries and documents into a shared space for nearest-neighbor search, while generators integrate retrieved passages with the query for context-aware responses.

\section{Traditional RAG}

% \textit{The building blocks:} \\
% \textit{Document chunking and preprocessing (text splitters, cleaning, metadata extraction)} \\
% \textit{Embedding generation (models like Sentence Transformers, OpenAI, etc.)} \\
% \textit{Vector store/database (FAISS, Weaviate, Milvus) for fast similarity search} \\
% \textit{Retriever module (dense, sparse, hybrid)} \\
% \textit{Generator module (LLM, e.g., GPT-4, Llama)} \\
% \textit{Reranker module to improve retrieval quality} \\
At the core of any RAG system lies a modular pipeline that orchestrates document preparation, retrieval, and generation. The process begins with \textbf{document chunking and preprocessing}, where raw texts (e.g. PDFs, HTML pages) are cleaned, normalized, and split into manageable passages. Early approaches employed fixed-length character or token windows; however, modern implementations increasingly leverage \textbf{recursive splitting heuristics}\cite{RecurrentChunking}\cite{SplittingHeuristics} to avoid excessively small or large segments and \textbf{semantic chunking} via clustering embeddings to respect topical boundaries. During this stage, metadata such as section headings and publication dates are also extracted to enhance downstream retrieval.

Next, each chunk is transformed into a vector representation in the \textbf{embedding generation} phase. Models such as Sentence-BERT \cite{SentenceBERT}, OpenAI's text-embedding-ada \cite{OpenAITextEmbedding}, or domain-specific encoders encode semantic content into high-dimensional dense vectors. In parallel, \textbf{sparse representations} (e.g., BM25\cite{BM25} term vectors) may be computed, and \textbf{hybrid embeddings} formed by concatenating dense and sparse signals, striking a balance between semantic understanding and keyword recall.

These embeddings are then indexed in a \textbf{vector store}, for example, FAISS \cite{FAISS}, Weaviate \cite{Weaviate}, or Milvus \cite{Milvus}, which exploits advanced data structures like inverted file systems (IVF)\ref{appendix:invertedFileSystem}, product quantization (PQ)\ref{appendix:quantizationTechniques}, and Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs (HNSW)\ref{appendix:HNSW} to support sub-linear nearest-neighbor search over millions of vectors.

During inference, the \textbf{retriever module} maps an input query into the same embedding space, retrieves the top-$k$ nearest chunks, and, when using hybrid methods, combines dense similarity scores with sparse term matches. Finally, the \textbf{generator module}, typically a decoder-only model like GPT-4 or an encoder-decoder such as T5, conditions on the concatenation of the original query and retrieved passages to produce a fact-grounded response. Optionally, a \textbf{reranker} refines the retrieval results: cross-encoder rerankers rescore chunks by jointly processing query and passage, contrastive rerankers optimize embedding spaces for relevance discrimination, and in-context reranking leverages the LLM itself via few-shot prompts to prioritize the most pertinent evidence \cite{Reranking}.

Beyond the basic pipeline, advanced RAG systems incorporate specialized \textbf{extraction} and\textbf{transformation} methods to handle diverse corpora. For scanned or image-based documents, OCR engines paired with layout parsers reconstruct both textual content and its structural context (tables, figures, headings). Entity recognition and metadata parsing further annotate chunks with semantic labels, such as named entities or technical concepts, enriching retrieval signals.

In the transformation phase, \textbf{text splitting} strategies have evolved from naive fixed-size windows to sophisticated techniques. Recursive splitters adjust boundaries based on punctuation or section markers, whereas \textbf{cluster-based semantic chunking} groups contiguous sentences by embedding similarity, yielding more coherent passages at the cost of increased computational overhead. Some systems even employ \textbf{LLM-guided chunking}, using a small language model to identify topic shifts, which maximizes recall for downstream retrieval despite higher latency.

Embedding strategies have also diversified. \textbf{Dense encoders} capture semantic nuance but may suffer from “drift” as corpora evolve; \textbf{sparse methods} like BM25 remain robust to unseen terms; \textbf{hybrid models} integrate both to achieve superior precision-recall trade-offs.Correspondingly, vector stores have been extended into \textbf{hybrid stores} that layer graph or metadata indices atop dense embeddings, enabling rich faceted queries and semantic filtering.

Retrieval optimizations include \textbf{query expansion}, automatically adding synonyms or contextually related terms via thesauri or small LLMs, and \textbf{dual-encoder architectures} that separately train query and document encoders to maximize retrieval fidelity. More recently, \textbf{contextual retrieval} techniques prefix or interleave document titles and pseudo-questions (generated by models such as HyDE) to surface more relevant passages.

% \textit{Challenges:} \\
% \textit{Hallucination and factuality: LLMs may ignore retrieved context or hallucinate} \\
% \textit{Retrieval quality: Embedding drift, poor chunking, context window limits} \\
% \textit{Scalability: Indexing and updating large corpora, latency} \\
% \textit{Multilingual and domain adaptation challenges} \\
Despite these advances, traditional RAG frameworks contend with several persistent challenges. Even when relevant passages are retrieved, LLMs may under-utilize them, reverting to memorized knowledge or hallucinating unsupported details, a phenomenon that undermines factuality. The quality of retrieval itself depends heavily on chunking strategy and embedding freshness; as corpora grow or drift over time, embeddings may become stale, necessitating frequent re-indexing. Scalability concerns loom large when indexing billions of tokens, as vector stores incur storage, latency, and cost overheads. Finally, most embedding models are pretrained on general-purpose English text, resulting in suboptimal performance on specialized domains or non-English languages without additional fine-tuning or multilingual adaptation.

These limitations motivate exploration of complementary approaches, most notably graph-based and multimodal RAG, which aim to bolster multi-hop reasoning, cross-modal grounding, and dynamic knowledge integration.

\section{Graph RAG}
% \textit{Purpose and potential:} \\
% \textit{Addresses limitations of vanilla RAG for multi-hop, global, or broad queries} \\
% \textit{Enables structured reasoning over entities and relationships} \\
% \textit{Supports modular, hierarchical, and community-based summarization} \\
While traditional RAG systems excel at single-hop factual retrieval, they often struggle with multi-hop reasoning, global summarization, and broad or compositional queries that require understanding across dispersed contexts. Graph RAG frameworks aim to address these limitations by introducing explicit structure into the retrieval pipeline, namely, via knowledge graphs that model entities, relationships, and their contextual interdependencies. By augmenting text with structured representations, these systems enable more interpretable, traceable, and compositional reasoning pathways. Moreover, the graph abstraction naturally supports modular, hierarchical, and community-based summarization, allowing information to be surfaced not just by semantic proximity but by its position within a larger conceptual topology.

A growing body of research has explored how to integrate graph-based representations into the RAG paradigm. GraphRAG \cite{GraphRAG} transforms unstructured corpora into knowledge graphs through entity and relation extraction, followed by community detection to identify densely connected subgraphs for hierarchical abstraction. This structure proves especially effective for global or overview-style queries, where conventional vector retrieval may underperform due to semantic dispersion.

In contrast, LightRAG \cite{LightRAG} adopts a lightweight dual-level retrieval architecture, combining local vector retrieval with global graph-based recall. Its incremental update mechanism allows it to incorporate new data without full reindexing, making it particularly well-suited for dynamic or continuously evolving knowledge bases.

PathRAG \cite{PathRAG} focuses on multi-hop relational reasoning by identifying traversable paths between entities across documents. To mitigate path explosion and redundancy, it introduces decay pruning, which filters paths based on both length and semantic coherence. The resulting paths not only yield more concise retrieval targets but also provide interpretable rationales, surfacing chains of evidence that mirror human reasoning.

Meanwhile, KG2RAG \cite{KG2RAG} emphasizes preprocessing and chunk expansion by leveraging existing or extracted knowledge graphs to organize and semantically expand retrieval contexts. This framework enriches retrieved chunks with related facts or entities, improving recall and facilitating implicit multi-hop grounding without requiring explicit reasoning in generation time.

% \textit{Notable techniques:} \\
% \textit{DRIFT method: Pruning based on path length and semantic decay} \\
% \textit{Late chunking: Efficiently incorporates new data without full reindexing} \\
Several technical innovations underpin the effectiveness of Graph RAG systems. The DRIFT search\cite{DRIFTSearch}, which is derived with a pruning process introduced in the context of PathRAG\cite{PathRAG}, attenuates the influence of long or semantically diffuse paths using a decay function, prioritizing concise, high-relevance traversal routes. Late chunking strategies, which defer text segmentation until after graph construction, enable systems to adaptively shape chunk boundaries around graph topologies or relation clusters, thereby preserving entity coherence and improving retrievability.

These methods collectively support dynamic and efficient reasoning, but they come at a computational cost. Graph construction typically requires intensive entity and relation extraction, often mediated by large LLMs, which makes the process both resource-heavy and error-prone. The quality of extracted graphs remains sensitive to prompt engineering and upstream NLP accuracy, with hallucinated or missing entities potentially corrupting the graph topology and impairing reasoning reliability.

% \textit{Challenges:} \\
% \textit{High upfront cost for graph construction (entity/relation extraction, LLM calls)} \\
% \textit{Sensitivity to prompt engineering and extraction quality (hallucinated or missing entities/edges)} \\
% \textit{Scalability: Graph size, path explosion, memory/computation for large corpora} \\
% \textit{Maintenance: Updating graphs as data changes} \\
From a systems perspective, Graph RAG raises significant scalability concerns. As the corpus grows, graph size and path complexity increase non-linearly, leading to memory and runtime bottlenecks. Path explosion, in particular, becomes a limiting factor in dense or ambiguous knowledge spaces. In parallel, graph maintenance, especially in environments with streaming or frequently updated data, remains an open challenge. Unlike vector indices, which can be incrementally updated, knowledge graphs may require re-normalization or even reconstruction to ensure consistency and integrity.

% \textit{Hybrid retrieval: Combining graph and vector-based methods} \\
% \textit{Multi-modal graph construction (images, tables, code)} \\
% \textit{Evaluation metrics for cross-modal and multi-hop reasoning} \\
% \textit{Other paths:} \\
% \textit{Explainability and trust in graph-based reasoning} \\
% \textit{Agentic workflows (planner/critic agents) for graph RAG} \\
% \textit{Real-time or streaming updates to graphs} \\
Research is actively exploring how to hybridize graph-based and vector-based retrieval, allowing systems to balance semantic fuzziness with structural exactness. Another promising frontier is multi-modal graph construction, where nodes and edges represent not only textual entities but also visual elements (e.g., diagrams), tabular data, or even code fragments. Such multimodal graphs could facilitate cross-domain reasoning and more faithful grounding in complex documents.

As these systems evolve, new evaluation metrics will be needed to assess performance in multi-hop, cross-modal, and graph-augmented settings, where traditional retrieval and generation scores may fall short. Interpretability remains a core concern as well: graph paths offer a natural substrate for explainability, enabling models to articulate the basis of their outputs.

Finally, Graph RAG is well-positioned to support agentic workflows, in which LLMs act not only as generators but as planners or critics, navigating the graph to construct or validate reasoning chains. Supporting such agents will require efficient APIs for real-time or streaming graph updates, enabling knowledge flows that are both reactive and grounded.


\section{Multimodal RAG}

Traditional RAG architectures are predominantly unimodal, assuming that the input corpus is composed solely of text. However, many real-world documents, such as scanned PDFs, scientific papers, forms, and diagrams, contain complex visual layouts, embedded figures, or non-textual cues that textual embeddings alone cannot adequately capture. Multimodal RAG aims to extend the retrieval-augmented generation paradigm to such heterogeneous data, integrating visual and textual modalities to support retrieval, reasoning, and generation over richly formatted or image-based content.

The earliest multimodal RAG systems rely on a pipeline that first applies optical character recognition (OCR) to extract text from images or scanned documents. This is typically followed by text chunking, layout parsing, and occasionally caption generation to describe figures or tables. While effective in some settings, this pipeline is slow, error-prone, and fragile when confronted with noisy inputs, such as poor-quality scans, handwritten text, or documents with intricate multi-column or nested layouts. Furthermore, semantic coherence can be lost during OCR, especially when visual cues like font size, spatial alignment, or figure boundaries are discarded.

% \textit{PaliGemma:} \\
% \textit{Vision-language model for image and text embedding} \\
% \textit{Uses SIGLIP with Pali3 for improved multi-modal retrieval} \\
To address these limitations, newer systems incorporate vision-language models capable of learning joint representations over both images and text. PaliGemma \cite{PaliGemma}, for instance, combines SIGLIP\cite{SIGLIP} (a vision transformer) with Pali-3\cite{Pali3} (a multilingual language model) to produce dense, cross-modal embeddings. This enables improved retrieval across image-text boundaries, allowing for more contextually aware and semantically grounded retrievals.

% \textit{ColPali:} \\
% \textit{Bypasses OCR, directly encodes document pages as images} \\
% \textit{Late interaction retrieval: Multi-vector image embeddings, fine-grained matching} \\
A more radical shift is embodied in ColPali\cite{ColPali}, which bypasses OCR entirely. Instead of converting documents into text, ColPali treats each page as a raw image and encodes it directly using a multi-vector image embedding scheme. These embeddings support late interaction retrieval, meaning that fine-grained relevance can be determined during inference rather than at index time. This strategy enhances alignment between query semantics and document visuals, particularly for complex documents that defy easy text extraction.

% \textit{Other approaches:} \\
% \textit{VDocRAG:} \\
% \textit{Dual-encoder for queries and document images, dynamic high-res image encoding, multi-hop reasoning, context-independent questions, OpenDocVQA dataset} \\
Several emerging frameworks push the boundaries of multimodal retrieval-augmented generation. VDocRAG\cite{VDocRAG} uses a dual-encoder architecture to jointly embed queries and document images, dynamically adjusting image resolution based on task complexity. It supports multi-hop reasoning across visual content and has been evaluated on OpenDocVQA, a benchmark for open-domain document visual question answering.

% \textit{ViDoRAG:} \\
% \textit{Multi-modal hybrid retrieval (visual + textual), dynamic result adjustment (GMM prior), agent-based generation (seeker, inspector, answer agent), ViDoSeek dataset} \\
ViDoRAG\cite{ViDoRAG} takes a hybrid approach, combining textual and visual retrieval with a dynamic adjustment mechanism based on Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM). It further introduces an agent-based generation framework, where specialized agents (e.g., seeker, inspector, answer agent) collaborate to process and synthesize multimodal evidence. This architecture, tested on the ViDoSeek dataset, demonstrates strong performance in scenarios requiring cross-modal coordination and reasoning from disparate sources.

% \textit{VisRAG:} \\
% \textit{Query-centric multi-modal retrieval, retrieval-augmented generation, modular and memory-efficient, strong on VQA benchmarks} \\
Similarly, VisRAG\cite{VisRAG} adopts a query-centric retrieval strategy, retrieving multimodal evidence based on relevance to the query rather than document-centric similarity. Its modular design emphasizes memory efficiency and component interchangeability, and it has shown competitive results on multiple visual question answering (VQA) tasks.

% \textit{mmGraphRAG:} \\
% \textit{Dual DB (graph + vector), incorporates object detection, spatial relationships, and image features} \\
Finally, mmGraphRAG introduces a dual-database architecture, combining a knowledge graph with a vector store, similarly to what has been done in HybridRAG\cite{HybridRAG}. It integrates object detection, spatial layout modeling, and image feature embeddings, creating a structured yet flexible framework for reasoning over visual documents. This design enables entity-centric, relational queries that span both textual and visual domains, unlocking richer and more compositional reasoning capabilities.

% Challenges and future directions
Despite recent advances, Multimodal RAG systems face persistent challenges. OCR pipelines remain brittle and domain-specific, while vision-language models require extensive pretraining and careful calibration to ensure cross-modal alignment. The computational overhead associated with high-resolution image encoding, multi-vector matching, and agent-based generation can be substantial, limiting real-time applicability. Furthermore, the lack of standardized benchmarks and metrics for evaluating multimodal RAG performance across tasks, such as layout understanding, table reasoning, or figure caption grounding, complicates model development and comparison.

Promising directions for future research include the development of cross-modal knowledge graphs, where visual objects, textual elements, and their relationships are explicitly represented and queried. Hybrid retrieval mechanisms that blend textual, visual, and structured representations may provide the flexibility needed for open-domain multimodal tasks. Another area of interest lies in streaming multimodal retrieval, where models adapt in real time to evolving visual content (e.g., dynamic dashboards, live documents).

More broadly, multimodal RAG systems offer a path toward explainable AI\cite{ExplainAI}, where retrieved evidence, whether textual, visual, or relational, can be explicitly surfaced and interpreted. This opens the door to interactive, agent-driven reasoning systems, capable not only of answering questions, but of demonstrating how and why a given response was generated.

\section{Metrics}
% \textit{Factuality, relevance, comprehensiveness, diversity, logicality, coherence} \\
% \textit{Retrieval accuracy, answer correctness, multi-hop reasoning} \\
A robust evaluation of RAG systems requires targeted metrics that reflect both retrieval performance and the quality of generated responses, particularly in multimodal and reasoning-intensive settings. This study employs a concise yet comprehensive set of metrics to ensure meaningful assessment: \\
\textbf{Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG)}\ref{appendix:nDCG} evaluates the quality of ranked retrieval results by rewarding the correct placement of relevant documents near the top of the list. It accounts for the graded relevance of retrieved chunks and penalizes lower-ranked relevant items, providing an effective measure of ranking quality in RAG pipelines. \\
\textbf{Context Recall}\ref{appendix:recall} measures the proportion of relevant evidence that was successfully retrieved. In high-stakes or imbalanced settings such as multimodal reasoning tasks, recall is critical, as it captures the model's ability to detect all relevant content, minimizing the risk of omitting essential information. \\
\textbf{Context Precision}\ref{appendix:precision} assesses the proportion of retrieved evidence that is truly relevant. It highlights how accurately the system avoids bringing in irrelevant or misleading documents. Precision is especially valuable in reducing noise in the retrieved context, which can negatively influence the generation phase. \\
\textbf{Faithfulness}\ref{appendix:faithfulness} measures the degree to which the generated answer adheres to the retrieved context. An answer is considered faithful if its claims can be directly inferred from the evidence provided. This metric involves extracting factual claims from the output and validating them against the source context, yielding a score between 0 and 1. \\
\textbf{Answer Relevance}\ref{appendix:relevance} evaluates the pertinence and completeness of the generated answer with respect to the user query. It involves generating multiple questions from the answer itself and comparing them to the original question using vector similarity (e.g., cosine or dot product). High similarity indicates that the answer effectively addresses the original query without redundancy or omission. \\
Together, these metrics offer a nuanced and rigorous framework for evaluating the effectiveness of multimodal RAG systems, balancing retrieval precision with output integrity and user relevance.

\section{Transformer Architecture}
\label{appendix:transformer}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.5\textwidth}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width = 8cm]{./figures/transformer.png}
  \end{center}
\end{wrapfigure}

The Transformer architecture is a deep learning model used for processing sequential data, particularly in natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision. It utilizes a self-attention mechanism, which allows the model to learn relationships between different parts of the input sequence. This architecture consists of an encoder and a decoder, both built from stacked layers of attention mechanisms and feed-forward networks. \\

Key Components and Functionality: \\
\textbf{Self-Attention}:
The core of the Transformer architecture, enabling it to weigh the importance of different parts of the input sequence when generating output.
\textbf{Encoder}:
Processes the input sequence and generates contextualized representations.
\textbf{Decoder}:
Generates the output sequence based on the encoder's output and its own attention mechanism.
\textbf{Positional Encoding}:
Since Transformers lack recurrence, positional encodings are used to capture the order of tokens in the input sequence.
\textbf{Multi-Head Attention}:
Allows the model to attend to different parts of the input simultaneously, capturing richer contextual relationships. \\

How it Works:
\textbf{1. Input}:
The model takes an input sequence, which can be text, images, or other data. 
\textbf{2. Embedding}:
The input sequence is converted into numerical vectors called embeddings. 
\textbf{3. Encoding}:
The encoder processes the embeddings, using self-attention and feed-forward networks to generate contextualized representations. 
\textbf{4. Decoding}:
The decoder uses the encoder's output and its own attention mechanisms to generate the output sequence. 
\textbf{5. Output}:
The model produces the desired output, which can be a translation, a summary, or another type of sequence. 

\section{Inverted File System}
\label{appendix:invertedFileSystem}
The Inverted File System (IVF) is a data structure used in approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search, particularly in vector databases and large-scale similarity search systems. It is widely used in libraries like FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity Search) to speed up the process of finding the nearest vectors in a high-dimensional space. \\

Core Concepts
\textbf{Coarse Quantization} (Clustering):
The dataset vectors are clustered using a method like k-means into nlist clusters (e.g., 1000). Each cluster center is a coarse centroid. Each data vector is assigned to its nearest centroid.
\textbf{Inverted Lists}:
Each cluster (centroid) maintains a list of the vectors assigned to it, this is the inverted list. Instead of storing the full vectors, some systems store residuals (difference between the vector and the centroid), or quantized versions of the vectors to save space.
\textbf{Querying}:
To find nearest neighbors for a query vector:
The query is first compared to the centroids.
The nprobe closest centroids are selected (e.g., 10).
Search is performed only within the corresponding inverted lists, reducing the number of comparisons. \\
The advantages of this data structure are a scalability to millions or billions of vectors, and a tunable tradeoff between speed and accuracy.

It works well with quantization techniques like PQ (Product Quantization) for further compression.
